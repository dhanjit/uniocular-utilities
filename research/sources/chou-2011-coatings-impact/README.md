# Ballistic impact resistance of selected organic ophthalmic lenses (Chou, Yuen & Dain, 2011)

**Citation:** Chou BR, Yuen GS-C, Dain SJ. "Ballistic impact resistance of
selected organic ophthalmic lenses." *Clinical and Experimental Optometry*.
2011;94(6):568–574.
**Identifiers:** PMID 21895769; DOI
[10.1111/j.1444-0938.2011.00651.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1444-0938.2011.00651.x).
**Access:** Paywalled (publisher returned 403).
**Verification:** Citation, methods and conclusions verified via the PubMed
abstract (2026-11-12). Per-brand fracture velocities (for example the Hoya
EYAS 1.60, Nikon 1.67, Phoenix and Trilogy values quoted in earlier notes)
are in the full text, which was not retrieved, and are **not** reproduced.

## Scope

Laboratory ballistic test of coated and uncoated mid-index lens materials.
Nominally plano lenses in three thicknesses, flat-edged to 50 mm, struck at
the center with a 6.35 mm steel ball; threshold fracture velocity found with
the ZEST staircase protocol.

## Key points

- Threshold fracture velocity generally increased with thickness, but
  **materials of the same thickness varied widely**; most between-material
  differences were significant.
- **Impact resistance increases non-linearly with center thickness.**
- The lens mounting matters: fracture velocities were significantly **lower**
  than in earlier studies where the lens edge was restrained.
- A **scratch-resistant coating reduced the impact resistance of CR-39**.
- The effect of an AR coating depended on the scratch coat beneath it;
  "cushion coatings" might enhance impact resistance.

## Learnings for this project

- "Mid-index" or "high-index" is not one material: two lenses of the same
  index and thickness can differ a lot. A generic grade on a sales sheet is
  not evidence for a specific lens.
- Thickness is a strong, non-linear lever, which supports asking for a stated
  minimum center thickness.
