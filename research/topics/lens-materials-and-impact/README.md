# Topic: Lens materials, refractive index and impact resistance

Which spectacle lens material, how thick, and with what coatings, for
someone whose one seeing eye is irreplaceable. This is the technical
companion to [eye-protection](../eye-protection/), which covers *whether* and
*when* to wear protection and why people stop wearing it.

Informational only. This is **not medical advice**. Lens choice belongs to
the wearer and their eye-care professional.

## Summary

- **Every guideline that covers adults with one good eye says to wear
  impact-resistant spectacles as a standing habit, not only for risky tasks.**
  The activity-specific wording in these guidelines is always about *extra*
  protection (goggles, face shields) on top of the everyday pair. The AAO's
  adult guideline names polycarbonate. Trivex is named by some secondary
  sources, not by the AAO guidelines.
- **The legal test for everyday lenses is weak.** The US FDA drop-ball test
  delivers about **0.2 J** and sets **no minimum thickness**. Almost any
  modern lens passes it, so "meets FDA" says little about protection.
- **Material matters more than anything else.** In independent lab tests,
  polycarbonate was the only material that withstood all tested projectiles.
  Trivex-type urethane (Hoya Phoenix, Younger Trilogy) performs well but below
  polycarbonate. CR-39, glass and "high-index plastic" shattered at energies
  lower than everyday hazards deliver. No independent test of the Mitsui MR
  high-index materials by name was found. The maker's own table rates its 1.74
  material only "OK" for impact, the same grade it gives CR-39.
- **Thickness matters next.** Polycarbonate shattered under the safety-standard
  steel ball only at **center thickness (CT) of 1.8 mm or less**, and
  perforated more often **below 2.2 mm**. Recurring recommendations are
  **2 mm for daily wear and 3 mm for sport** (AAO, written for children). A
  "thinner lens" upsell trades this margin away.
- **At moderate myopia, high index buys little.** At −3.25 D and the same CT,
  a 1.74 lens edge is only about **0.4–0.5 mm** thinner than polycarbonate in a
  typical frame, while having lower rated impact resistance and an Abbe value
  no better than polycarbonate's and well below Trivex's.
- **Coatings cost some impact resistance.** Anti-reflection (AR) coating
  lowered Trivex-type lens breakage speed by about 9–13% and made CR-39 "too
  fragile" for industrial use. Coated polycarbonate and Trivex still beat
  uncoated brittle materials.

**Reading across the table:** no source limits everyday impact-resistant
spectacles to risky activities. The "only when doing X" wording always
describes **extra** protection on top of the everyday pair. The explicit
"always" or "all waking hours" wording comes from EyeWiki, an AAO patient page,
the Amblyopia PPP (for children) and Boss et al. The AAO's adult guideline
says "recommended for all individuals with good vision in only one eye"
without a time qualifier. Only polycarbonate is named by the AAO. No guideline
endorses a high-index material (1.60, 1.67 or 1.74) for a one-eyed wearer.

Sources:

- FDA rule text: [21 CFR 801.410 (Cornell LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/801.410).
  It also lets a physician or optometrist waive impact-resistant lenses, in
  writing, if they "will not fulfill the visual requirements" of a patient.
  It sets no numeric thickness anywhere.
- Z87.1 high-velocity and high-mass test descriptions (1989 edition) are from
  [vinger-woods-2000-rx-safety-eyewear](../../sources/vinger-woods-2000-rx-safety-eyewear/).
  Under that edition, Rx lenses had to pass only a 1-inch ball drop and were
  "permitted to fail with the high-mass and high-velocity tests". Its
  thickness rules were: plano lenses that pass high velocity 2.0 mm, plano
  lenses that fail it 3.0 mm, lenses over +3.00 in the most-plus meridian
  2.5 mm, all other Rx lenses 3 mm.
- Z87.1-2020 Rx thickness rules (3.0 / 2.5 / 2.0 mm) and the "Z87-2+" marking
  are from an optician study guide ([open-exam-prep NOCE guide](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/noce/laws-regulations-standards/ansi-z87-1-fda-safety-regulations)).
  Secondary sources disagree on whether 2.0 mm high-impact is allowed for
  materials other than polycarbonate and Trivex. The standard text itself was
  not available.
- EN ISO 16321 levels, scope and transition date: vendor summary
  ([uvex blog](https://www.uvex-safety.com/blog/new-protective-eyewear-standards-en-iso-163212022/)).
  Beware summaries that give old EN 166 "B" as 80 m/s: B was 120 m/s. It
  maps by name to the new level D, which is 80 m/s.

**Takeaway:** the everyday-lens floor (~0.2 J) is roughly a fifth of the
basic occupational test and far below real hazards. A lens that "meets FDA"
has been shown only to survive a light drop at its thinnest production form.

## 4. Center thickness

For a minus lens the center is the thinnest point, so **CT is the number that
governs impact performance**.

| Recommendation or finding | Figure | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |

| AAP/AAO, children who cannot fit sports goggles | "3-mm polycarbonate lenses in ANSI Z87.1 frames", explicitly "not optimal" | [AAP/AAO 2013](https://www.aao.org/education/clinical-statement/protective-eyewear-young-athletes) |

**"Thinner lens" sales advice.** The usual upsell for high index, and for
"thin" polycarbonate, is a thinner, lighter lens. For a minus prescription
that means a thinner center, which is exactly where the margin above lies.
Unless the order states a CT, the lab's default applies. An optician forum
reports polycarbonate and Trivex being surfaced as thin as about 1.0 mm
(Low-quality evidence: [OptiBoard](https://www.optiboard.com/forums/forum/optical-forums/general-optics-and-eyecare-discussion-forum/1002191-minimum-material-thickness/page2)).
No adult-specific AAO or AOA thickness figure was found. The 2 mm / 3 mm
numbers come from children's guidance and occupational standards.

## 5. Index vs thickness at moderate myopia

### Edge thickness at −3.25 D

Thin-lens approximation, derived from lens sag:

> edge thickness ≈ CT + y² × |F| ÷ (2000 × (n − 1))
>
> with y = distance from the optical center to the edge (mm), F = lens power
> (dioptres), n = refractive index.

Same CT (2.0 mm) for every material, so only the index differs.
y = 25 mm is roughly the edge of a 50 mm lens centered on the pupil.
y = 30 mm allows for a larger frame or decentration.

| Material | n | Edge minus CT at y = 25 mm | Edge at y = 25 mm | Edge at y = 30 mm |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CR-39 | 1.498 | 2.04 mm | 4.0 mm | 4.9 mm |
| Trivex | 1.53 | 1.92 mm | 3.9 mm | 4.8 mm |
| Polycarbonate | 1.586 | 1.73 mm | 3.7 mm | 4.5 mm |
| MR-8 | 1.60 | 1.69 mm | 3.7 mm | 4.4 mm |
| MR-7 / MR-10 | 1.67 | 1.52 mm | 3.5 mm | 4.2 mm |
| MR-174 | 1.74 | 1.37 mm | 3.4 mm | 4.0 mm |

A check with exact spherical surfaces (front +2.00 D) agrees within 0.1 mm.
This is the project's own calculation from a textbook formula. Real lenses
differ with base curve, aspheric design and frame shape.

**What it shows:** going from polycarbonate to 1.74 at the same CT saves about
**0.4 mm** of edge at y = 25 mm and **0.5 mm** at y = 30 mm. Going from CR-39
to 1.74 saves under 1 mm. The bigger lever is frame size and centration:
moving the edge from y = 30 to y = 25 mm saves 0.6–0.9 mm in every material.
High-index lenses only look much thinner when they are *also* made with a
thinner center. A 1.74 lens at a typical CT of about 1.1 mm (an assumption,
not sourced) would have a 2.5 mm edge at y = 25 mm, against 3.7 mm for
polycarbonate at 2.0 mm CT. Most of that 1.2 mm difference comes from the
thinner center, not the index.

**Is 1.74 justified at −3.25 D?** On this evidence, not for a one-eyed
wearer: it buys well under 1 mm of edge at equal CT, has the lowest impact
grade in its maker's own range, and is not named by any guideline. This is
the project's inference, consistent with general dispensing practice that
reserves the highest indices for high powers.

### Abbe value and color fringing

Lateral color (transverse chromatic aberration, TCA) is the color fringing
seen when looking through a lens away from its optical center:

> prism (Δ) = c (cm) × |F| (D)   (Prentice's rule)
>
> lateral color (Δ) = prism ÷ Abbe value
>
> (formula as given by [Whitney, 20/20 Magazine, Sept 2015](https://www.2020mag.com/article/does-material-abbe-value-influence-your-patients-vision))

Whitney (Carl Zeiss Vision; chair of ANSI Z80.1) puts the "just observable
error" for lateral color "in the neighborhood of 1/8 prism diopter"
(0.125 Δ), and says about 0.25 Δ "may be bothersome to some patients". That is
expert opinion from industry experiments, not a peer-reviewed threshold. He
also writes that below 2 D "lateral color will not be noticeable".

| Material | Abbe value (source) | TCA at 5 mm | 10 mm | 15 mm | Distance where 0.125 Δ is reached |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CR-39 | 58 (Whitney); 57 νe [vendor, Mitsui] | 0.028 Δ | 0.056 Δ | 0.084 Δ | 22 mm |
| Trivex | 43–45 (Whitney 45; Calvi 2026 review 43–45) | 0.036 Δ | 0.072 Δ | 0.108 Δ | 17 mm |
| MR-8 1.60 | 41 νe [vendor, Mitsui] | 0.040 Δ | 0.079 Δ | 0.119 Δ | 16 mm |
| MR-174 1.74 | 32 νe [vendor, Mitsui] | 0.051 Δ | 0.102 Δ | 0.152 Δ | 12 mm |
| MR-7 / MR-10 1.67 | 31 νe [vendor, Mitsui] | 0.052 Δ | 0.105 Δ | 0.157 Δ | 12 mm |
| Polycarbonate | 30 (Whitney); 28–30 νe [vendor, Mitsui] | 0.054 Δ | 0.108 Δ | 0.163 Δ | 11.5 mm |

Calculated at −3.25 D with the Abbe value in the first cell. Mitsui quotes νe
(the e-line); most other figures are νd. The difference does not change the
ranking. Vendor values are from Mitsui's [MR lineup page](https://jp.mitsuichemicals.com/en/special/mr/lineup/)
and poster, both marked "not guaranteed as specifications".

**What it shows:** polycarbonate and the 1.67 / 1.74 materials are
essentially the same for color fringing. Trivex and MR-8 are noticeably
better, and CR-39 best. So moving from 1.74 to polycarbonate costs nothing
chromatically, and Trivex improves on both. With one eye, there is no second
image to average out fringes, which slightly raises the value of good
centering and a higher Abbe value (project inference).

## 6. Practical recommendation for a uniocular adult

*This section is the project's inference from the evidence above, not a
clinical guideline, and not medical advice.*

- **Material:** polycarbonate or Trivex, for the everyday pair and any
  sunglasses, and for **both** lenses, including the lens in front of the
  non-seeing eye. (Guidelines specify the material for the pair; none
  addresses the two lenses separately.) Polycarbonate has the strongest
  independent evidence against blunt and BB-type impacts. Trivex is lighter
  (density about 1.1 vs 1.2 g/cm³, per the
  [Calvi 2026 review](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules31203680)), has a better
  Abbe value, and does not "oil-can" out of the frame. In the one rig that
  tested both, Trivex-type lenses broke while unexposed 2 mm polycarbonate
  could not be broken ([chou-2015-uv-ageing-impact](../../sources/chou-2015-uv-ageing-impact/)).
- **Center thickness:** ask for it in writing. **At least 2.0 mm** for daily
  wear. For sport and high-risk tasks, add rated protection (ASTM F803 sports
  goggles, Z87+ / EN ISO 16321 safety eyewear) rather than relying on 3 mm
  street spectacles. The AAP/AAO call 3 mm polycarbonate in Z87.1 frames a
  "not optimal" fallback.
- **Avoid for daily wear:** CR-39, glass, and 1.60 / 1.67 / 1.74 high-index
  plastics; "thinnest possible" upgrades that cut CT below 2 mm; contact
  lenses as the only correction (they are "not protective"); rimless or
  drilled mounts (retention depends on the frame — more than 40% of safety
  frames with removable lenses broke or lost lenses under realistic impacts in
  [vinger-woods-2000-rx-safety-eyewear](../../sources/vinger-woods-2000-rx-safety-eyewear/)).
- **Coatings:** AR coating is a trade-off, not a ban. It reduced Trivex-type
  breakage speed by about 9–13% and severely weakened CR-39; with
  polycarbonate it lowered resistance to sharp missiles, most at 2 mm. If AR
  is wanted, keep the material impact-resistant and the CT at 2 mm or more.
  Coating effects depend on the coating stack ([chou-2011-coatings-impact](../../sources/chou-2011-coatings-impact/)
  found "cushion coatings" might help).
- **Replace aged lenses:** UV exposure lowered impact resistance in every
  material tested, even without visible damage
  ([chou-2015-uv-ageing-impact](../../sources/chou-2015-uv-ageing-impact/)).
- **Ignore "meets FDA" as a protection claim:** it is a ~0.2 J test with no
  thickness rule.

## Implications for the project

- The eye-protection manual needs a short, concrete **"what to ask the
  optician"** card: material (polycarbonate or Trivex), CT ≥ 2.0 mm, both
  lenses, frame retention, and what to decline.
- A utility could calculate edge thickness and lateral color for a given
  prescription and frame, showing that high index rarely pays off at low and
  moderate powers for this group.

## Open questions

- No independent impact test of Mitsui MR-8, MR-7, MR-10 or MR-174 by name.
- No adult-specific thickness recommendation from AAO or AOA.
- No quantified data on rimless or semi-rimless mounting with polycarbonate
  or Trivex.
- No study of color-fringe tolerance in monocular compared with binocular
  viewing.
- Independent, published high-velocity comparison of Trivex and polycarbonate
  at matched thickness and coating.
